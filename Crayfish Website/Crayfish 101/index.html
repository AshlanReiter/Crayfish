The History of Redclaw

From the freshwater environments of northern Australia to modern aquaculture.

Australian Origins

The Australian Redclaw crayfish, scientifically known as Cherax quadricarinatus, is a freshwater crayfish native to tropical northern Australia and southern New Guinea. In Australia, its native range includes parts of Queensland and the Northern Territory.

The species naturally occurs in freshwater environments, including slow-moving streams, billabongs and other suitable tropical habitats.

From Native Species to Aquaculture

Redclaw became increasingly important to aquaculture because of characteristics such as relatively fast growth, natural reproduction in ponds and a relatively simple life cycle.

These characteristics made the species attractive to farmers looking for a freshwater species that could be raised for food and commercial production.

Development of Farming

Research into hatchery and nursery production helped establish methods for producing juvenile redclaw under controlled conditions.

Research has since examined broodstock management, spawning, juvenile production, stocking density, feeding, shelter and pond production.

From Australia to the World

Redclaw is now cultured in many parts of the world. Its adaptability has contributed to its popularity in aquaculture, but responsible containment is important because introduced populations have become established outside their native range.

Responsible aquaculture means preventing accidental escape and never releasing farmed crayfish into natural waterways.

What Is Redclaw?

Australian Redclaw is a large freshwater crayfish belonging to the family Parastacidae. It is sometimes marketed using names such as "freshwater lobster" or "blue lobster", but it is biologically a crayfish rather than a marine lobster.

The species is particularly recognizable for its blue-green to green coloration and, in mature males, the distinctive reddish patch found on the outer part of the major claw.

Body Structure

  • Cephalothorax — contains the head and thorax, including the eyes, antennae, mouthparts and walking legs.
  • Abdomen — the segmented rear portion of the body.
  • Claws — used for feeding, defense and interaction with the surrounding environment.
  • Tail fan — helps the crayfish move through the water.

Growth Through Molting

A crayfish cannot simply expand its hard outer shell as its body grows. Instead, it periodically sheds its old exoskeleton in a process called molting.

Immediately after molting, the new shell is soft. During this period the crayfish is more vulnerable to injury and attacks from other crayfish.

How to Identify Redclaw

Australian Redclaw can be identified by several characteristic features.

  • Blue-green to green coloration is common.
  • Mature males have a distinctive reddish patch on the outer margin of the major claw.
  • The carapace has four longitudinal ridges, a feature reflected in the species name quadricarinatus.
  • The animal has large claws and a segmented abdomen ending in a tail fan.

How to Take Care

Good redclaw culture starts with clean water, suitable shelter, proper feeding and regular observation.

01 — Water Quality

Water quality directly affects feeding, growth, molting, reproduction and survival. Important factors include temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, ammonia, nitrite and hardness.

AMMONIA Keep Low
NITRITE Keep Low

Water requirements can vary according to the culture system and life stage. Monitor the actual conditions of the system instead of relying on a single number.

02 — Shelter

Crayfish need places where they can hide, especially during and after molting when their shells are soft.

Suitable structures can include PVC pipe sections, mesh bundles and other safe shelters with appropriate openings.

03 — Feeding

Redclaw are opportunistic feeders and can consume naturally available food as well as suitable formulated feeds.

Feed should be adjusted according to animal size, biomass and culture conditions. Excess food should be avoided because decomposing feed can negatively affect water quality.

04 — Stocking Density

Stocking density affects competition, growth and the amount of space available to each animal. Increasing density can reduce average harvest size and growth rate.

More crayfish does not automatically mean more successful production.

A productive system needs a balance between stocking density, food, shelter, water quality and available space.

05 — Molting

During molting, a crayfish sheds its old exoskeleton and develops a new one. The animal is especially vulnerable while its new shell is still soft.

  • Provide adequate shelter.
  • Avoid unnecessary handling.
  • Watch for aggressive interactions.
  • Maintain stable water conditions.

Proper Handling

Handle crayfish calmly and carefully to reduce injury and unnecessary stress.

Before Handling

  • Prepare the destination container first.
  • Use suitable water.
  • Prepare nets or other equipment.
  • Minimize handling time.
  • Avoid unnecessary disturbance.

Why Care Is Important

Crayfish can react quickly when threatened. Their claws are used for defense and can injure a handler if the animal is grabbed carelessly.

Rough handling can also cause damage to the animal, particularly around the legs, claws and soft areas following a molt.

DO

  • Handle calmly.
  • Keep handling time short.
  • Support the animal properly.
  • Use suitable nets or containers.
  • Return the crayfish to suitable water quickly.

DON'T

  • Grab the claws.
  • Squeeze the body.
  • Drop the animal.
  • Handle freshly molted crayfish unnecessarily.
  • Leave the crayfish out of water longer than necessary.

Extra Care After Molting

A newly molted crayfish has a soft exoskeleton and is particularly vulnerable to injury and attacks from other crayfish. Whenever possible, allow it to remain undisturbed and give it access to adequate shelter.

Moving Crayfish

When transferring crayfish between containers or culture systems, prepare the receiving environment before catching the animals. Avoid unnecessary exposure, dropping or rough handling. For repeated farm operations, appropriate nets and containers can reduce direct handling.

Selecting Broodstock

Breeding begins with healthy broodstock. Farmers can select mature animals that are active, healthy and free from obvious physical damage.

Controlled hatchery research has demonstrated successful spawning and juvenile production under suitable environmental conditions.

Mating & Spawning

Redclaw can reproduce naturally under suitable culture conditions. Reproductive activity is influenced by environmental factors such as temperature and day length.

Controlled research has demonstrated successful spawning under warm temperatures and controlled photoperiods.

Egg Carrying

After spawning, the female carries the eggs beneath her abdomen. The eggs remain attached while they develop through several stages.

The incubation period can vary according to environmental conditions, particularly temperature.

Juveniles

Young crayfish are vulnerable because of their small size and limited ability to compete with larger individuals.

Appropriate food, shelter, water quality and stocking density are important during nursery production.

From Juvenile to Market Size

Once juveniles reach an appropriate stage, they can be moved into grow-out systems. Growth time depends on temperature, food availability, stocking density, water quality, management and the desired harvest size.
